Where space rocks land

Meteorites rain down on the whole planet more or less evenly. The record of them does not. Of the {{source.n|int}} meteorites in the Meteoritical Society's catalogue as NASA publishes it, {{regions.Antarctica.pct|pc}} were picked up on Antarctic ice and {{hot.pct|pc}} in hot deserts. The {{source.falls|int}} that someone actually saw fall tell a different story again: they follow people.

Every day, rocks from space reach the ground somewhere on Earth. Most fall into the sea, onto forests or fields, and are never seen again. The ones we know about were either seen to fall, and then picked up (a "fall"), or simply spotted on the ground later (a "find"). The Meteoritical Society has kept the official list for decades, and NASA publishes a copy with the place, year, class and weight of each one.

Put that list on a map and it looks like a map of meteorites. It is really a map of searching. Antarctica, where ice flows carry stones to bare blue ice fields and expose them, and the hot deserts of North Africa and Oman, where dark stones sit for thousands of years on pale ground, account for {{icedesert.pct|pc}} of all records. The witnessed falls, which do not depend on searching, crowd into Europe, India and Japan, where there have long been many people to look up and someone to write it down.     02 · Falls and findsMost meteorites were found on ice or sand

    Only {{source.falls|int}} of the {{source.n|int}} meteorites ({{source.falls_pct|pc1}}) were seen to fall. The other {{source.finds|int}} are finds, and most come from a handful of places. Antarctica alone has {{regions.Antarctica.n|int}} ({{regions.Antarctica.pct|pc}}), the Sahara {{regions.Sahara.n|int}} ({{regions.Sahara.pct|pc}}), Arabia, mostly Oman, {{regions.Arabia.n|int}} ({{regions.Arabia.pct|pc}}), and the Atacama and Australian deserts {{regions.Other hot deserts.n|int}}. The whole rest of the world has {{regions.Rest of the world.n|int}} ({{regions.Rest of the world.pct|pc}}), and {{regions.Not known.pct|pc}} cannot be placed at all.

    03 · Ice and sandThe great meteorite rush after 1969

    Before 1969 the record grew slowly, by chance finds and witnessed falls. The first Antarctic meteorite, {{antarctic.first.name|raw}}, turned up in {{antarctic.first.y|raw}}, and only {{antarctic.pre1969|int}} more followed before {{antarctic.yamato.y|raw}}, when a Japanese team found {{antarctic.yamato.n_first|int}} near the Yamato Mountains and realised the ice was concentrating them. Systematic searches by Japanese and American teams followed, and the name Yamato alone now covers {{antarctic.yamato.n|int}} meteorites. From the 1980s hunters and dealers began combing the Sahara and Oman. Finds peaked in the {{finds_time.peak_dec|raw}}s, with {{finds_time.peak_n|int}} in one decade; {{finds_time.since1970_pct|pc}} of all dated finds are from 1970 or later.

    Antarctic finds are also small. The median Antarctic meteorite weighs {{mass.median_ant|f1}} g, the median Saharan one {{mass.median_sahara|int}} g, and the median find from the rest of the world {{mass.median_rest_find|int}} g: on bare ice or sand, a stone the size of a grape is easy to spot, while in a field it is just another pebble. Many of these stones are also pieces of the same fall, scattered across the ice and named separately until someone pairs them, so the counts overstate the number of separate falls.

    A map of meteorites is really a map of where people have looked for them.

    04 · Witnessed fallsFalls follow people

    Witnessed falls are the fairest test, because nobody goes out looking for them: they are seen, heard and then searched for. If every fall on land were noticed, each country's share of falls would match its share of land. Instead, Europe, with {{continents.Europe.area_pct|pc}} of the land, has {{continents.Europe.falls_pct|pc}} of the falls ({{continents.Europe.ratio|f1}} times its share); Asia, home to India's {{countries.IND.falls|int}} falls, has {{continents.Asia.ratio|f1}} times; North America almost exactly its share; Africa {{continents.Africa.ratio|f1}}, South America {{continents.South America.ratio|f1}} and Oceania {{continents.Oceania.ratio|f1}}.

    The link with people is clear but loose. Across the {{countries.n_big|int}} countries larger than 20,000 km², falls per square kilometre rise with people per square kilometre (Spearman's rank correlation {{countries.spearman|f2}}); among the {{countries.n_big_falls|int}} that have at least one fall, the correlation of the two on a log scale is {{countries.pearson_log|f2}}. Population here is today's rough estimate from Natural Earth, not the population when each stone fell, and history matters as much as headcount: Europe and Japan have centuries of written records, newspapers and museums that wanted the stones.

    Over time, witnessed falls rose from {{falls_time.d1800|int}} in the 1800s to {{falls_time.d1860|int}} in the 1860s and a peak of {{falls_time.peak_n|int}} in the {{falls_time.peak_dec|raw}}s, as populations, literacy, newspapers and scientific interest grew. Then they levelled off, at about {{falls_time.since1950_mean|int}} a decade since 1950, even though the world's population grew by {{falls_time.pop_growth_pct|int}}% between the 1960s and the 2000s. The oldest falls in the record are Nogata in Japan (dated {{falls_time.oldest.0.y|raw}}) and Narni in Italy ({{falls_time.oldest.1.y|raw}}).

    05 · Heavy metalThe giants are all iron, and all finds

    The heaviest meteorite on record is Hoba in Namibia, {{heaviest.0.t|f1}} tonnes, still lying where it was found in {{heaviest.0.y|raw}}. Next come Cape York in Greenland ({{heaviest.1.t|f1}} t, many pieces) and Campo del Cielo in Argentina ({{heaviest.2.t|f1}} t). All ten of the heaviest are irons, and {{mass.top10_found|int}} of the ten are finds. The heaviest witnessed fall is Sikhote-Alin in the Russian Far East ({{heaviest_falls.0.t|int}} t, {{heaviest_falls.0.y|raw}}), followed by Jilin in China ({{heaviest_falls.1.t|int}} t, {{heaviest_falls.1.y|raw}}) and Allende in Mexico ({{heaviest_falls.2.t|int}} t, {{heaviest_falls.2.y|raw}}).

    Iron meteorites are rare among falls ({{classes.Iron.falls_pct|pc1}}) but make up {{classes.Iron.rest_pct|pc}} of finds outside Antarctica and the deserts. They rust slowly, look and feel unlike any local rock, and set off metal detectors, so in fields and forests they are the ones people notice and keep. On ice and sand, where every dark stone stands out, the mix of finds looks much more like the falls: mostly ordinary chondrites. The rarest stones of all, {{mars_moon.martian|int}} from Mars and {{mars_moon.lunar|int}} from the Moon, come {{mars_moon.desert_ice_pct|pc}} from ice and desert.

    06 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. NASA's Meteorite Landings table ({{source.n_rows|int}} records), compiled from the Meteoritical Society's Meteoritical Bulletin Database, from a copy mirrored on GitHub and checked cell by cell against a second, independent mirror (identical except that three old finds with no year in the first copy have a garbled year in the second) and a third, five-column one. It has the name, class, recovered weight, fall or find, year and coordinates of each meteorite. The table stops in {{source.last_year|raw}}; the live Bulletin has grown a great deal since. I dropped one record, Meridiani Planum, which was found on Mars by a rover. Years are as given (the table stores 1 January of the year); one year in the future was set to missing.

    Places. {{source.unlocated|int}} records have no coordinates or sit at exactly 0°, 0°, which means unknown. For these I took the region from the collection area in the name (for example "Northwest Africa 869" or "Yamato 791197") when at least 80% of that area's located meteorites share one region ({{source.region_from_name|int}} records). Antarctica is everything south of 60°S. The hot deserts are rough boxes: the Sahara (15°N to 34°N, 17.5°W to 34°E), Arabia (12°N to 31°N, 35°E to 60°E), the Atacama, and the Australian deserts (including the Nullarbor). Present-day countries are from Natural Earth.

    Even spread. Meteorites arrive at a rate that is close to uniform over the globe, so I compare each country's falls with its share of the world's land area. Population is Natural Earth's recent estimate (POP_EST), used only as a rough guide; it is not the population at the time of each fall. About {{ocean.falls_per_land|f1}} times as many falls land in the sea as on land, and none of those are in the record.

    What this is not. It is not a count of meteorite falls. Antarctic and desert totals are inflated by fragments of single falls named separately; finds have no date of fall at all, only a date of finding; and weights are the total recovered, sometimes from many pieces. The record also reflects who was searching: national Antarctic programmes, commercial hunters in the Sahara and Oman, and the museums that classified their stones.
